## 1. Scope & Purpose

In 2005, three researchers — Donald Dutton, Ehor Boyanowsky, and Michael Bond — published a paper with an unnervingly matter-of-fact title: *Extreme Mass Homicide: From Military Massacre to Genocide*. Behind the title sat a provocation. The scholarly literature had long treated genocide as a subject for historians and political scientists, and battlefield massacres as a subject for military law and the psychology of combat stress. Dutton and his colleagues argued that this division of labor was hiding the most important finding in plain sight: **the same psychological machinery drives both.** A company of soldiers destroying a village in a morning and a state dismantling a people over years are, at the level of mechanism, close relatives.

That claim is the spine of this synopsis. If it is right, it changes what we should look for — not a special breed of perpetrator, but a reproducible *situation*; not inexplicable outbreaks of evil, but an escalation process with stages, signatures, and points of failure.

> The question is not "what kind of person does this?" but "what kind of situation makes almost any person capable of it?"

The paper — and therefore this synopsis — matters to three audiences at once:

*   **Psychology.** The paper synthesizes what social psychology has painstakingly established since Milgram: obedience to authority, dehumanization, group polarization, and the erosion of moral restraint are not exotic pathologies but standard human equipment under the wrong management. It challenges purely dispositional explanations — the comforting theory that atrocities are committed by "them," the disturbed few — by tracing how *situational transitions* convert ordinary people into killers, sometimes in hours.
*   **Genocide studies.** By placing massacre, genocide, and political slaughter inside one comparative frame, the paper sharpens the field's typology instead of blurring it. The differences between a genocide and a massacre — in intent, organization, and duration — become analytically useful precisely because the underlying commonalities are mapped first.
*   **Policy and prevention.** Escalation that proceeds in stages can, in principle, be interrupted at stages. The paper's process model yields concrete early-warning indicators — elimination rhetoric in official speech, militia formation, the selective disarming of a target group — that translate scholarship into something a monitoring mission or a foreign ministry can actually use.

One boundary should be drawn honestly at the outset. This is a synopsis of a work of *explanation*, and explanation always risks being mistaken for excuse. Understanding how situations manufacture perpetrators does not diminish their guilt; it locates the additional guilt of those who built the situation. That distinction is held firmly throughout what follows.
