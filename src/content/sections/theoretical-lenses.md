## 3. Theoretical Lenses

In the summer of 1961, in a basement laboratory at Yale, a psychologist named Stanley Milgram asked ordinary residents of New Haven to deliver what they believed were increasingly painful electric shocks to a stranger, on the say-so of a man in a gray lab coat. Psychiatrists he consulted beforehand predicted that perhaps one in a thousand — the rare sadist — would proceed to the maximum voltage. Sixty-five percent did. Milgram had designed the study with the Holocaust explicitly in mind, and its lesson has been replicated, refined, and resisted ever since: the willingness to harm on instruction is not a rare defect. It is a standard human setting, waiting for the wrong operator.

Dutton, Boyanowsky, and Bond build their framework on that foundation. The question that organizes their theoretical review is not what is *wrong* with perpetrators, but what is *done to* — and *around* — otherwise ordinary people to make mass killing performable. Several mechanisms recur across every case in this synopsis:

*   **Obedience to authority.** Killing at scale is almost always killing under orders — or under the sincere belief that orders exist. Authority does more than compel; it *absorbs responsibility*. The soldier at the ditch and the clerk at the deportation list can each tell themselves the decision was made elsewhere. Rwanda's organizers understood this intuitively: the killing was framed as *work*, announced by officials, organized like a civic duty.
*   **Dehumanization.** Empathy is the brake, so the brake is removed first — linguistically. Tutsis became *inyenzi*, cockroaches; Armenians a "cancer" in the body of the empire; class enemies in Cambodia mere instruments of foreign powers. Once the target is verbally converted into vermin, disease, or contamination, violence is reframed as hygiene. No case study in Part II skipped this step.
*   **Moral disengagement.** Albert Bandura's term for the suite of self-exonerations that let people commit harm while keeping their self-image intact: euphemistic language ("resettlement," "special treatment," "search and destroy"), diffusion of responsibility across the group, displacement of responsibility onto superiors, blaming of victims. Perpetrator testimony from every era reads like a catalogue of these maneuvers.
*   **Group polarization and conformity.** Groups drift toward extremity. In a unit or militia where hard-liners set the tone, each member's private doubts stay private, and the observable consensus ratchets toward violence. Refusal, meanwhile, carries visible costs — from ostracism to, in Rwanda, death. Christopher Browning's study of a German reserve police battalion found that even when refusal was *explicitly permitted*, fewer than one man in five stepped out of line: comradeship did the coercing.
*   **Diffusion of responsibility.** A firing squad exists so that no one fired the shot. Mass atrocity divides its labor — list-makers, transporters, cordon troops, shooters, buriers, propagandists — until each participant holds a fragment too small, in his own eyes, to constitute the crime.

> Ordinary men are not recruited because they are cruel. They are made cruel by the situation they are recruited into — and the situation is built deliberately.

Two further moves distinguish the paper's framework. The first is the authors' proposal of a **"forensic ethology"** — studying human collective violence the way ethologists study animal behavior: empirically, comparatively, looking for triggers, thresholds, and recurring sequences rather than moral categories. Massacres, in this view, leave behavioral signatures — the rage of a unit that has taken casualties from an invisible enemy, the crowd dynamics of a militia at a roadblock — that can be catalogued and, potentially, anticipated.

The second is the framework's center of gravity: the rejection of **trait explanations** in favor of **situational transitions**. Screening studies of perpetrators, from Nuremberg onward, keep delivering the same unwelcome result — most are psychiatrically unremarkable. What changes is not the person but the situation: a border crossed, a uniform issued, an order given, a first killing survived. Each transition renormalizes the next step. This is the paper's most consequential claim, because traits cannot be prevented, but situations can be. Dispositional theories end in the courtroom; situational theories end in early warning.

None of this dissolves responsibility — the men who built the situations knew what they were building, and many who stood inside them still refused. Hugh Thompson landed his helicopter; villagers across Rwanda hid their neighbors. The mechanisms explain how the crowd moves. The exceptions prove that standing still remained possible — which is exactly why the organizers of atrocity work so hard to make it costly.
