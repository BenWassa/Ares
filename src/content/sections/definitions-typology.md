## 2. Key Definitions & Typology

Words are where prevention starts, and where denial hides. In 1994, officials with lawyers at their elbows performed linguistic contortions to avoid uttering the word “genocide” about Rwanda — the U.S. State Department famously permitted only the phrase “acts of genocide” — because the word carries legal obligations, and obligations have costs. Sixty years earlier, the crime itself had no name at all: Raphael Lemkin had to invent the word *genocide* in 1944 because, as Churchill put it, the world was confronting “a crime without a name.” Definitions in this field are not academic housekeeping. They determine what the world admits is happening, and what it is therefore obliged to do.

The authors’ umbrella construct, **extreme mass homicide**, deliberately steps back from the legal battlefield. It names a comparative family of large-scale, intentional, group-perpetrated killing of the defenseless. The Ares 2.3 prototype now stores the three working typology definitions in the validated reading contract rather than repeating them in this manuscript and a separate guided version.

The typology earns its keep in the case studies. My Lai and El Mozote are treated as massacres: concentrated violence in villages by military units. The Armenian case is used as the systematic genocidal variant. The Holodomor and the Cambodian Killing Fields are discussed through political slaughter while preserving the complications that resist a single label. Cambodia’s revolution also destroyed Cham Muslim and Vietnamese minorities on genocidal terms, and Bosnia’s ethnic cleansing was adjudicated, at Srebrenica, to have crossed into genocide.

That is the deeper point of the framework. The boxes matter — legally and morally, intent is central — but the *pathway into the boxes* can still expose shared mechanisms. A precise typology makes the comparison more rigorous rather than more rhetorical: it lets Ares ask what historically unlike cases may share while naming exactly where they differ.
