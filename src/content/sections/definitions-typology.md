## 2. Key Definitions & Typology

Words are where prevention starts, and where denial hides. In 1994, officials with lawyers at their elbows performed linguistic contortions to avoid uttering the word “genocide” about Rwanda — the U.S. State Department famously permitted only the phrase “acts of genocide” — because the word carries legal obligations, and obligations have costs. Sixty years earlier, the crime itself had no name at all: Raphael Lemkin had to invent the word *genocide* in 1944 because, as Churchill put it, the world was confronting “a crime without a name.” Definitions in this field are not academic housekeeping. They determine what the world admits is happening, and what it is therefore obliged to do.

The authors’ umbrella construct, **extreme mass homicide**, deliberately steps back from the legal battlefield. It names the whole family of large-scale, intentional, group-perpetrated killing of the defenseless — and then distinguishes its members carefully:

| Term               | Definition                                                                                                        | Key Characteristics                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| :----------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Massacre**       | The deliberate and indiscriminate killing of a large number of defenseless people, often in a short period.         | Typically localized and fast — hours or days. Often erupts inside warfare or civil unrest; perpetrators are usually military or paramilitary units. Aims to terrorize or eliminate an immediate “threat” rather than to destroy an entire people. |
| **Genocide**       | Acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group.      | Systemic and sustained. State-sponsored or state-condoned, with organized logistics — lists, deportations, camps, killing sites — and an ideological justification. The target is the group *as such*: its future, its culture, its children.        |
| **Political Slaughter** | Large-scale killing by a state or political movement against its own citizens, targeted by political category rather than ethnicity. | Targets “class enemies,” dissidents, or whole social strata; proceeds through purges, engineered famine, forced labor, and execution. Distinguished from genocide by the absence of intent to destroy an ethno-racial group as such — though the death tolls can be equal or greater. |

The typology earns its keep in the case studies. My Lai and El Mozote are massacres: single mornings, single villages, soldiers as perpetrators. The Armenian Genocide, the Holocaust’s template, is the systematic variant: a bureaucracy aimed at a people. The Holodomor and the Cambodian Killing Fields are political slaughter — Stalin’s target was drawn by class and nation together, the Khmer Rouge’s by an ideology that could make eyeglasses a capital offense. And some cases refuse to stay in one box: Cambodia’s revolution also destroyed its Cham Muslim and Vietnamese minorities on plainly genocidal terms, and Bosnia’s “ethnic cleansing” was adjudicated, at Srebrenica, to have crossed into genocide.

That is the deeper point of the framework. The boxes matter — legally and morally, intent is everything — but the *pathway into the boxes* is disturbingly shared. A precise typology makes the comparison rigorous instead of rhetorical: it lets us say exactly what a hundred-day genocide by machete and a four-year slaughter by famine have in common, and exactly where they differ.
